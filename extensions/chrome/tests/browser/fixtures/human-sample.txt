I spent most of last winter rebuilding the shed at the bottom of the garden, and I still do not know whether it was worth it. The old one had a felt roof that let water through in three places, so every autumn I would find another bag of compost turned to soup. My neighbour, who has strong opinions about most things, told me to knock it down and buy a plastic one. He was probably right. I did not listen.

The frame came apart more easily than I expected. Two of the corner posts had rotted so far down that I could push a screwdriver straight through them, which is the sort of discovery that makes you glad you never stored anything heavy on the shelves. The rest fought back. I spent a Saturday morning with a crowbar and a bad temper and got about a third of the way through before the rain came in sideways and I gave up and went inside to make tea.

What surprised me was how much of the old timber was fine. The cladding boards on the south wall were dry, straight and perfectly usable once I had pulled the nails out. There is a particular smell to old pine that has been kept dry for twenty years, slightly sweet, a bit dusty, and I found myself keeping boards I had no plan for simply because throwing them away felt wasteful. My wife pointed out that the pile in the driveway had become its own problem. She was also right.

The new frame went up over three weekends in February. I used treated timber this time and set the posts on concrete pads rather than straight into the soil, which is the mistake the previous owner made and which cost me the whole winter. Levelling the base took far longer than building the walls. I borrowed a laser level from a friend and still managed to be eight millimetres out at one corner, which I discovered only when the door refused to shut.

I fixed the door by planing the bottom edge, which is the sort of solution that would horrify a joiner but has held up fine for a year now. The roof is corrugated bitumen sheet, screwed down with washers, and so far it has not leaked once. The compost is dry. The tools have somewhere to live. The pile of salvaged pine is still in the driveway, and I have started telling people it is for a raised bed.

Would I do it again? Probably not in February. There were mornings when my hands were too cold to hold a screw and I stood there swearing at a battery drill that had given up in the night. But there is something about a building you put up yourself, however slightly out of square, that a flat-pack never gives you. Every time the door sticks slightly on a damp day I know exactly why, and I know exactly which corner to blame.
